Executive Summary

The product is strong. The cultural moment is real. What Diesel doesn't yet have is a building that matches the renaissance.

Glenn Martens joined Diesel in 2020 and rebuilt the brand's creative authority in three years. The Iceberg in Harajuku is that statement: a five-floor structure in the world's most design-literate retail market, designed to bring the ninety percent of Diesel's identity that has always existed below the surface into the light. The building turns that depth into a climb, a journey of self-discovery where visitors strip away social-media performance floor by floor until commerce becomes the outcome, not the offer.

02The Problem

Japan's luxury consumers read for depth, and ninety percent of Diesel has never been made spatially present.

A standard flagship displays product to an audience that buys the story behind the object, the craft evidence within it, and the cultural context that makes it worth possessing. A retail environment that shows product without showing the brand that made it is a missed conversation with exactly the audience most capable of having it. But the surface gap is only the symptom. The deeper enemy is the pose.

Diesel is
against the pose.

In 2025, self-expression has become the most carefully managed performance in the world. The algorithm decides what's brave. The feed decides what's authentic. Even the “weird” looks are weird in pre-approved ways: rebellious within acceptable parameters, optimized for approval from people who aren't in the room.

Diesel's enemy isn't conformity. The brand has always known conformity is a boring target. The real enemy is fake nonconformity, the three-hour “effortless” fit, the “I don't care what people think” outfit engineered for 50K likes. The performance of a self that hasn't been decided yet.

Diesel has always made clothes for people who have actually decided. The Tokyo flagship is built to help people get there.

The Cultural Context
Harajuku — Then & Now
1980s – 2010s
The Original
Permission Zone

Harajuku was Tokyo's designated rupture, the street where honne (the true self) was allowed to show in a culture that elsewhere required the mask. The kids on Takeshita Street dressed for each other, for the physical moment, for no audience that wasn't present. It was the most authentic expression culture Japan had produced in decades.

Now
Colonised
By The Feed

The Takeshita Street kids are largely gone. Instagram replaced the street. The looks are still wild, but they're performing for an algorithm, not for each other. The authentic space that Harajuku once was has been consumed by the same performative self-expression that Diesel is against globally.

Diesel Tokyo is a reclamation. The flagship exists where the original permission zone existed, to rebuild the space where you find out who you are before you tell everyone about it.

03The Insight

The building is
the product.

What Diesel has always had, and what the Martens era restored, is a depth most fashion brands do not possess. The archive exists. The craft philosophy exists. The history of the brand as a challenger to the industry's own pretensions exists.

None of it has ever been made spatially present at the scale the brand warrants. Japanese consumers don't need that story explained, they need a building that makes it inhabitable.

The Payoff
And the product is the argument for everything Diesel has ever stood for, the archive, the craft, and the conviction.
·Gravitational Pull

The first flagship
optimised for self-knowledge.

Every other brand spent the last decade optimising stores for content, better lighting for fit checks, photo walls, engineered “moments.” Diesel Tokyo does the opposite.

It is designed to strip away the apparatus of performed self-expression and force a more fundamental question: who are you when nobody's watching?

The Strategic Thesis
We don't need you to see it. We need you to feel it.
Find out who you are first. Post later.
·The Central Provocation

The retail
floors are
phone-free.

Not a wellness gimmick. Not a status signal. A genuine provocation: you've been dressing for your phone so long, you've forgotten what it feels like to dress for yourself.

At entry, your phone is checked via a beautifully designed coat-check system: brass hooks, numbered slots, a tactile ritual. In return, you receive a brass token. The last branded object you'll hold before you enter. The first thing you photograph when you leave.

The Stakeholder Question

CEO / Shareholder Objection
“If nobody's on their phone, how does this go viral?”
The Answer
We're not stopping people from posting about us. We're making sure that when they do, they actually have something to say.

People don't post about seamless experiences. They post about experiences that made them feel something they didn't expect. The content from inside Ghiaccio, earned after an hour phone-free, is worth more than the post from a brand activation with a flower wall. Scarcity creates desire. Friction creates stories.

The Brass Token. Stamped with the brand's founding ethos. Milled from solid brass. Yours to keep. The first object in the building is also the only one with no price tag, because the decision to enter is the most valuable thing you'll do here.
The Three-Act Content Engine
Friction creates stories

The phone-free floors don't kill the content pipeline. They upgrade it into three distinct acts, each generating a different kind of organic reach.

1
Before You Enter

The phone check-in is the first content. The brass token, the ritual of surrender, the decision to go in: people document themselves giving up their phone more enthusiastically than they document themselves shopping.

2
The Absence

Nothing generates more content than a conspicuous absence from content. “I just spent an hour phone-free in Diesel Tokyo and I don't know how to describe what happened but—” That sentence writes itself in a hundred thousand ways. You cannot buy this.

3
Ghiaccio — The Reward

The top floor is fully phone-enabled. By then, the person posting has spent an hour making real decisions without algorithmic input. The content from Ghiaccio will read differently: slower, more considered, more authentic. Because we engineered the conditions to produce it.

·Customer Journey

Five floors.
One ascent.

The building turns shopping into a climb: each floor earns the next, with its own material register, phone policy, and strategic role. Walk it from the street to the apex.

01 Explore F1 · Gallery Retail +
Enter without mirrors or performance
Phone-free floor

The ground floor runs at full commercial intensity in a gallery setting. Lighting is calibrated for the body, not the phone. There are no mirrors on the floor. The cash wrap is carved from rough-hewn volcanic stone, and the first Diesel campaign ever banned by an advertising body hangs framed above the entrance. Feel the brand before you perform for it.

Strategic Intent
Close the gap between creative output and physical environment. The first thing a customer feels is the disconnect between what they expected from Diesel and what they walked into. That gap is the brand moment.
1 / 9
Ground floor · gallery retail volume & fixture kit
02 Discover F2 · The Edit +
What version of yourself are you not wearing yet?
Phone-free floor

One wall. Forty pieces. No racks. A stylist, not a sales associate, opens with a single question. Richer materials replace raw concrete: dark walnut, hammered brass, black marble with gold kintsugi joinery. Without a phone to check, the only input is the fabric, the stylist, and the question they asked at the door.

Strategic Intent
Reposition Diesel within the premium tier in the Japanese consumer's mind, designed for the intimate, considered decision rather than the status purchase.
1 / 3
The Edit · considered selection
03 Craft F3 · Denim Lab +
The Destruction Ceremony
Phone-free floor

A glass-walled theater of craftsmanship visible from the floor below. Bring in your most worn pair of jeans. An artisan documents the wear patterns as a forensic record of your life in denim, then repairs or transforms them. The documentation is yours to keep. Your jeans as autobiography.

Strategic Intent
Monetise craft and embed loyalty at the product level. A garment someone helped make isn't returned. Maps directly to Japanese monozukuri, the art of making things with care.
1 / 4
Denim Lab · craft theater
04 Learn F4 · Café 1978 +
Heritage, provocation, third-place culture
Phone-free floor

Named for the founding year. A coworking café built around a curated library of the brand's visual history. At the centre: The Banned Shelf, every campaign Diesel has ever had pulled, censored, or officially complained about, displayed with the complaint letters mounted alongside. Brand as a place to stay, not just a place to buy.

Strategic Intent
Convert non-purchasers into brand loyalists through provocation-as-education. Everyone who stays for a coffee leaves knowing exactly who Diesel is and what it has been willing to lose.
1 / 3
Café 1978 · archive and social volume
05 Transcend F5 · Ghiaccio +
The social apex. Phones earned back.
Phones permitted

The building's apex and the deepest point of the iceberg. A monolithic bar constructed from molded glass, internally cooled to appear perpetually frozen. Phones are fully permitted here because by this point, the person posting has earned it. Whatever they say now is real. Content that reads slower and more real than anything captured on the floors below.

Strategic Intent
Reward the customer for going further. The climb is the proof of investment, and the only place phones are welcome is the one place a post is earned.
1 / 5
Ghiaccio · ice bar and dining floor

Why this matters for retail: the ascent above is the spatial logic for everything that follows, the design language, the fixtures, and the returns are all in service of getting a customer to climb.

04Business Benefits

What the
building returns.

A flagship of this scale is not primarily a retail unit. It is an infrastructure investment in brand equity, the kind that pays out across every market, every product line, for years after opening. The direct returns below are the measurable floor. The real value is what happens to the brand's position globally when this exists.

One number worth holding: the brands that have committed flagship infrastructure to Japan have reported Japan as a top-three market for global profitability within 36 months. The entry cost is real. So is the ceiling.

Return on Investment
Return 01
Brand Equity
Global Prestige Signal
Success in Japan's discerning market is internationally recognised as a quality benchmark. A Harajuku flagship of this scale communicates to press, wholesale buyers, and consumers across every market simultaneously that Diesel operates at the prestige tier.
Return 02
Market Access
Asian Market Gateway
Japan's cultural influence creates an amplification effect across the broader Asian market. Tokyo is the beachhead. Seoul, Shanghai, and Singapore are the consequence.
Return 03
Loyalty Infrastructure
Highest-LTV Consumer Base
The Destruction Ceremony, the Ghiaccio table, the brass token: loyalty mechanics, not amenities. Japan's propensity for deep brand loyalty makes this the highest-lifetime-value customer base Diesel can cultivate.
Return 04
Content Quality
Earned Organic Reach
The phone-free floors upgrade content rather than suppress it. Diesel gets better organic distribution by engineering the conditions for authenticity. This is the content strategy built into the architecture.
Return 05
Proof of Concept
The Expansion Template
This flagship demonstrates that Diesel can execute at prestige-tier retail globally under the Martens creative vision. If the Iceberg works in Harajuku, the brief for Seoul, Shanghai, and London is already written. The investment here underwrites every subsequent expansion decision for the next decade.

The Proposition

Don't open a store.
Open the space Harajuku forgot it had.

Diesel's creative momentum is real and finite. The window to make a physical statement that matches the cultural moment is open now, and it will close. The Iceberg is not a retail expansion. It is a reclamation: of Harajuku, of authentic self-expression, of the version of “For Successful Living” that has always meant deciding who you are before performing it.
